Reproductive Physiology

Blubber Endocrinology & Reproductive Status

I developed and validated a non-invasive blubber-based pregnancy test for humpback whales — enabling detection of reproductive status from biopsy samples without ever needing to observe a calf. Progesterone, cortisol, and thyroid hormones extracted from blubber reveal the reproductive dynamics of recovering whale populations across multiple ocean basins.

Pallin et al. 2018 Pallin et al. 2024 Pallin et al. 2018b

Climate & Ecosystem

Krill Availability & Antarctic Baleen Whale Health

In the rapidly warming Antarctic Peninsula, shifts in sea ice alter krill abundance — with direct consequences for the whales that depend on them. My research links environmental variability, foraging ecology, and body condition to reveal when "surplus" krill is no longer sufficient to sustain whale population growth and reproductive success.

Pallin et al. 2023 Bierlich et al. 2022

Stress Physiology

Anthropogenic Stressors & Glucocorticoids

Blubber cortisol integrates chronic stress over weeks to months — a powerful window into the sub-lethal impacts of shipping, noise, and climate. I leveraged the COVID-19 "anthropause" as a natural experiment, documenting how sudden reductions in ocean noise and vessel traffic measurably reduced whale stress hormones in Monterey Bay and along the Antarctic Peninsula.

Pallin et al. 2022

Population Ecology

Demography, Connectivity & Photo-ID

Combining genetics, photo-identification, and biomarkers across 13+ years and thousands of animals, I reconstruct population structure and migratory connectivity of humpback and Antarctic minke whales spanning multiple ocean basins. My demographic work revealed the remarkable recovery of ice-obligate mysticetes and quantified how migratory fidelity shapes genetic structure over four decades.

Pallin et al. 2022 Marcondes et al. 2021

Conservation Science

Whales, Krill Fisheries & Adaptive Management

Baleen whales are the largest krill consumers in the Antarctic ecosystem — yet they are entirely absent from CCAMLR's Ecosystem Monitoring Program (CEMP). As recovering whale populations re-occupy the Southern Ocean and the commercial krill fishery proposes expanding catch limits by nearly 4×, the collision course between conservation and industry is accelerating. A single fishing vessel can harvest up to 1,000 metric tons of krill per day — roughly the foraging equivalent of 288 humpback whales. My ongoing work aims to establish the first whale-focused CEMP monitoring site, delivering pregnancy rate data and real-time physiological indicators directly into CCAMLR's management decision cycle. The goal is straightforward: ensure that krill fishery management accounts for its largest and least-counted predator — and that the management keeps pace with both ecosystem change and population recovery.

Beyond the Lab

Science for Everyone

The most urgent findings in ocean science are too important to remain behind paywalls. I'm committed to bringing marine biology into classrooms, galleries, and the public square.

"Science is a way of thinking much more than it is a body of knowledge." But knowledge locked away in journals doesn't change the world — connection does.

Whales are among the most charismatic animals on Earth, yet most people have never encountered the scientific evidence that shapes decisions about their protection. From the legacies of commercial hunting to the cascading effects of a warming Southern Ocean, the stories embedded in blubber and bones are profound — and urgently public. I believe scientists have a responsibility to meet people where they are: in schools, in museums, in the news, and in conversation.

Over more than a decade, I have partnered with artists, educators, journalists, NGOs, and policymakers to communicate marine science in ways that are honest, accessible, and lasting. The work below represents those efforts — from the front pages of the New York Times to elementary school classrooms in Minnesota.

Featured Project · IAS, UC Santa Cruz · 2025–2026

An Aesthetics of Resilience:
Weather and the Whale

What does climate change feel like? At UCSC's Institute of the Arts and Sciences, a landmark collaboration between scientists, artists, and curators brought together long-term data on whale physiology and behavior with immersive artistic installation — making the invisible rhythms of ocean biology visible, emotionally resonant, and urgent for general audiences.

As a Contributing Scientist, I helped translate years of field research into an exhibition language accessible to anyone. The project culminated in Weather and the Whale, an exhibition catalog published by University of Minnesota Press — documenting the science, the art, and the irreplaceable conversations that happen when both inhabit the same room.

Education & Community
🏫

K-12 Science Education

Through Polar-ICE and Data Nuggets (Rutgers University), I created curriculum connecting real Antarctic field datasets to K-12 classrooms across the country. I've also served as lead judge and research mentor at the Santa Cruz County K-12 STEAM Expo, and co-chaired a regional science fair in Minnesota.

🌊

California Ocean Alliance

As a contributing researcher and mentor with COA, I help develop the Marine Mammal Scientist in Training (MMST) program for local high school students — giving the next generation of ocean scientists their first hands-on research experience aboard real field vessels in the Monterey Bay.

🎓

Undergraduate Mentoring

I have mentored 14+ undergraduate researchers at UCSC, Duke, and partner institutions, with a strong commitment to supporting students from underrepresented communities. Several mentees are now pursuing graduate degrees in marine science and conservation biology.
